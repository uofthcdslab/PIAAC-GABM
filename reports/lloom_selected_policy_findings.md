# Themes in Responses to Selected Canadian AI Policy Proposals

## Executive summary

This report presents an exploratory qualitative analysis of model-generated responses associated with PIAAC-derived personas for four proposed Canadian AI policies:

1. Accessibility and inclusion in AI
2. Consumer privacy reform
3. AI-related employment and training
4. Indigenous leadership in AI

Across these policies, respondents generally saw potential benefits but treated policy commitments as insufficient on their own. Four cross-cutting messages emerged from the selected LLooM concepts and their illustrative excerpts:

- **Implementation matters as much as policy design.** Standards, rights, employment targets, and commitments to community leadership were often viewed positively, but respondents questioned whether they would translate into accessible programs and concrete support.
- **Training needs to be practical and tailored.** Respondents repeatedly recommended hands-on learning designed around different skill levels, occupations, languages, and community contexts.
- **Trust depends on participation and safeguards.** Community involvement, clear guidance, feedback mechanisms, privacy protections, cultural sensitivity, and data governance were described as conditions for responsible adoption.
- **Unequal access could reproduce existing inequalities.** Limited infrastructure, technology access, digital skills, time, and financial resources were recurring concerns, particularly for marginalized workers and Indigenous communities.

These are thematic findings, not estimates of how frequently each view occurs. LLooM was used to discover concepts from samples of responses; the responses were not subsequently scored against those concepts across the full dataset.

## Data and method

The source dataset contains approximately 2,180 model-generated responses per policy, each linked to a persona constructed from PIAAC characteristics. Every response includes a policy rating, a rationale, and suggestions for improving the proposal.

LLooM was run separately on the `Rationale` and `Suggestions` fields for each policy. For each policy-field combination, the procedure:

- drew a reproducible sample of 250 responses;
- used inductive concept discovery in batches of 10;
- selected six active concepts; and
- retained example response IDs for interpreting each concept.

The analysis used random seed 42. This report focuses on four of the 20 policies and discusses a subset of their active concepts. Quotations were checked against the corresponding `induction_sample.csv` files. Excerpts are shortened for readability; omissions are marked with ellipses. Response IDs are retained to support traceability.

> **Interpretive note:** The excerpts below are outputs from simulated personas, not quotations from human research participants. They illustrate the concepts identified by LLooM and should not be described as public opinion or population-level evidence.

## 1. Accessibility and inclusion in AI

### Policy context

The proposal would promote a national standard for accessible and equitable AI, address accessibility barriers in AI systems, and apply Gender-Based Analysis Plus across AI policy, skills development, innovation, and governance.

### Rationale themes

#### Standards offer a foundation for more equitable AI

Respondents saw value in a national standard because it could establish common expectations for accessible AI and signal that AI systems should serve people beyond technically confident users. This support was conditional: standards needed to be accompanied by infrastructure, training, and attention to the barriers people encounter in practice.

> “By promoting a national standard on accessible AI, it’s addressing something crucial—making sure that AI systems are not just for the tech-savvy. … [N]ot everyone has equal access to the necessary infrastructure or training to use AI effectively.”  
> — Illustrative response PIAAC-5304

#### Formal commitments may not translate into implementation

The distinction between announcing a standard and making it effective was central. Respondents identified skills gaps, mistrust, weak engagement, and uneven digital experience as factors that could limit the policy's reach.

> “I worry that merely setting a standard won't automatically translate into effective implementation. The complexities and barriers in real-world AI adoption—like the technical skills gap and the general mistrust many have towards AI technology—may not be fully addressed.”  
> — Illustrative response PIAAC-4417

### Suggestion themes

#### Provide practical, community-based digital-literacy support

Respondents recommended local, hands-on programs focused on everyday uses of AI. They also emphasized multilingual resources, mentorship, and ongoing support rather than one-time or highly technical instruction.

> “These programs should be hands-on and focus on practical, everyday applications of AI, rather than technical jargon that may alienate users like myself. Additionally, providing resources in multiple languages … could help bridge the learning gap.”  
> — Illustrative response PIAAC-559

#### Include affected communities in developing and monitoring standards

Accessibility was framed as a participatory process. Workshops and forums could surface barriers that technical experts might miss, while accountability measures could help convert commitments into observable changes.

> “This could include workshops or forums that allow people like me, who might not be experts but are affected by AI, to share their views and experiences. … [E]stablishing clear accountability measures … could help ensure that the commitments made are followed through with real action.”  
> — Illustrative response PIAAC-8632

### Policy implication

An accessibility standard is most likely to be credible when paired with funded delivery mechanisms: community partnerships, practical training, multilingual materials, user feedback, measurable accessibility criteria, and public reporting on implementation.

## 2. Consumer privacy reform

### Policy context

The proposal would recognize privacy as a fundamental right, strengthen individual control over personal information, protect children's information, and address inappropriate uses of personal data.

### Rationale themes

#### Privacy protection can create trust in digital systems

Respondents linked stronger privacy rights and personal control to confidence in digital platforms and, indirectly, willingness to adopt AI. At the same time, they saw trust as dependent on whether consumers and organizations could understand and exercise their responsibilities.

> “Recognizing privacy as a fundamental right and strengthening individual control over personal information are commendable steps. These changes lay the groundwork for greater trust in the digital landscape, which is crucial for fostering AI adoption.”  
> — Illustrative response PIAAC-9498

#### Complexity and unclear guidance could become adoption barriers

Respondents worried that new privacy rules could be difficult for individuals to navigate and burdensome for organizations to implement. The concern was not necessarily opposition to regulation; it was that unclear or complex requirements might weaken implementation and slow responsible innovation.

> “Companies might struggle to adapt and implement the changes effectively without clear guidelines, which could slow down technological advancement, contrary to the intent of modernization.”  
> — Illustrative response PIAAC-10367

### Suggestion themes

#### Treat privacy literacy as part of AI literacy

Privacy rights only provide meaningful control if people can understand them. Respondents recommended workshops, accessible guides, online resources, and support channels for both consumers and businesses.

> “I suggest implementing robust education and support programs aimed at both consumers and businesses to help them understand their rights and responsibilities concerning data privacy. This could involve workshops, online resources, and the creation of accessible guides.”  
> — Illustrative response PIAAC-6981

#### Deliver multilingual education through community settings

Some responses proposed using community centres and multilingual resources to reach groups that may face linguistic or institutional barriers. This connects privacy reform to inclusion rather than treating compliance as solely a legal or organizational issue.

> “Workshops in community centers, especially in areas with high immigrant populations, could demystify these technologies. Also, ensuring that resources and information are available in multiple languages … [would make] the digital landscape more accessible.”  
> — Illustrative response PIAAC-992

### Policy implication

Privacy reform could support AI adoption if rights are accompanied by plain-language guidance, multilingual public education, proportionate compliance support, and accessible mechanisms through which people can ask questions or raise concerns.

## 3. AI-related employment and training

### Policy context

The proposal would support up to 90,000 AI-related employment and work-placement opportunities by 2031 and expand employer-led AI training for mid-career workers, including people in skilled trades.

### Rationale themes

#### Employment targets do not resolve concerns about displacement

Respondents welcomed the prospect of new jobs but distinguished the number of opportunities created from workers' ability to access them. Fear of displacement and uncertainty about skill requirements could persist even in a growing AI labour market.

> “The potential benefit of creating 90,000 employment opportunities sounds great … but I also know that just having jobs isn't enough. Many people, especially those in skilled trades, might fear that AI will make their jobs obsolete or that they won’t have the skills needed to keep up.”  
> — Illustrative response PIAAC-5707

#### Training accessibility depends on workers' starting points

Respondents described differences in confidence, digital experience, and opportunity to undertake formal learning. Uniform programs could therefore advantage people who are already technically confident while leaving others behind.

> “Without proper guidance and tailored training that considers different skill levels, there’s a risk that the initiative could leave behind those of us who are not already tech-savvy.”  
> — Illustrative response PIAAC-10459

### Suggestion themes

#### Co-design training with employers, unions, and workers

Partnerships were proposed as a way to make programs relevant to real jobs. For skilled trades in particular, respondents favoured applied instruction that builds on existing expertise rather than generic AI courses.

> “Create partnerships between the government, trade unions, and employers to tailor AI training specifically for skilled trades. … [P]ractical training that incorporates our existing skills would bridge the gap between AI literacy and our work.”  
> — Illustrative response PIAAC-8102

#### Build mentorship and feedback into program delivery

Respondents recommended continuing support alongside formal instruction. Mentorship could reduce anxiety and provide individualized help, while participant feedback could identify gaps and improve programs over time.

> “Creating a mentorship program could help bridge the knowledge gap for those of us who may feel anxious about new technologies. … [E]stablish feedback mechanisms that allow participants to voice their concerns and experiences so that the programs can be continuously improved.”  
> — Illustrative response PIAAC-190

### Policy implication

The policy should be evaluated not only by placements or enrolments but also by who can participate, whether training matches workplace needs, whether workers receive follow-up support, and whether programs reduce—rather than intensify—employment insecurity.

## 4. Indigenous leadership in AI

### Policy context

The proposal would support Indigenous-led AI initiatives involving languages and cultural expression and enable Indigenous communities to shape AI applications involving language, land management, and cultural heritage.

### Rationale themes

#### Indigenous leadership can make AI more relevant and empowering

Respondents associated Indigenous-led development with cultural relevance, community authority, and increased engagement. Leadership was understood as more than consultation: communities would guide how AI is developed and determine which applications are meaningful.

> “By prioritizing Indigenous-led AI initiatives, it not only respects our languages and cultural expression but also empowers our communities to guide AI development in ways that are meaningful to us.”  
> — Illustrative response PIAAC-8002

#### Unequal resources and culturally insensitive systems could reproduce disparities

Support for the proposal was tempered by concerns about infrastructure, technical expertise, and access to training. Respondents also warned that AI applications could cause harm if cultural sensitivity were treated as an optional feature rather than a design requirement.

> “Access to technology and training is uneven, and without a strong support system, the potential benefits of this policy can easily be overshadowed by these challenges. Cultural sensitivity and awareness need to be integral to any AI applications.”  
> — Illustrative response PIAAC-1042

### Suggestion themes

#### Pair Indigenous-led training with Indigenous data sovereignty

Respondents connected technical capacity with governance. Community-led workshops could demystify AI, while a data-sovereignty framework could give communities control over cultural and linguistic materials used in AI systems.

> “Community-based workshops and training sessions led by Indigenous experts … could help demystify AI and provide hands-on experience with digital tools. Additionally, creating a framework for data sovereignty would ensure that Indigenous communities have control over their cultural and linguistic materials.”  
> — Illustrative response PIAAC-2713

#### Address technology access and establish ongoing dialogue

Affordable devices and connectivity were presented as prerequisites for participation. Respondents also recommended forums and early collaboration between developers and Indigenous leaders so that communities can shape the technology, assess its effects, and revise initiatives as conditions change.

> “Ensuring access to affordable technology could lower barriers for those who might feel left behind. Additionally, creating forums where community members can voice their concerns about AI and participate in shaping how it can be used would build trust.”  
> — Illustrative response PIAAC-4992-5

### Policy implication

Meaningful Indigenous leadership requires resources and decision rights as well as representation. Funding, connectivity, Indigenous-led technical training, community control of data, culturally grounded safeguards, and continuing governance roles should be treated as core program components.

## Cross-policy synthesis

| Cross-cutting theme | Accessibility and inclusion | Consumer privacy | Employment and training | Indigenous leadership |
|---|:---:|:---:|:---:|:---:|
| Practical and tailored training | ✓ | ✓ | ✓ | ✓ |
| Community participation or partnership | ✓ | ✓ | ✓ | ✓ |
| Trust, rights, or safeguards | ✓ | ✓ | ✓ | ✓ |
| Clear implementation guidance | ✓ | ✓ | ✓ | ✓ |
| Infrastructure or technology access | ✓ | — | — | ✓ |
| Multilingual or culturally relevant delivery | ✓ | ✓ | — | ✓ |
| Feedback and ongoing evaluation | ✓ | ✓ | ✓ | ✓ |
| Worker transition and job security | — | — | ✓ | — |
| Community authority over data or development | — | — | — | ✓ |

The comparison points to a common implementation chain:

> **Policy commitment → accessible resources → practical learning → meaningful participation → trusted adoption**

Breaking any link in this chain could limit the effectiveness of the proposals. Training without access may not reach intended groups; access without guidance may not build confidence; and adoption without participation or safeguards may weaken trust.

## Limitations

Several limitations should guide interpretation:

1. **The responses are simulated.** They were generated for PIAAC-derived personas and are not statements made by PIAAC respondents or members of the Canadian public.
2. **The analysis is exploratory.** LLooM discovered concepts from 250 sampled rationales and 250 sampled suggestions per policy. Concepts capture potentially important patterns but do not establish prevalence.
3. **Themes can overlap.** A single excerpt may reflect several concepts, such as training, access, trust, and community participation.
4. **Concept labels require interpretation.** LLooM generated related labels such as “digital skills training,” “AI literacy programs,” and “training needs.” This report groups such ideas analytically rather than treating every label as a distinct phenomenon.
5. **No population inference is warranted.** Neither the simulated personas nor the selected concepts should be used to estimate Canadian attitudes without additional validation and an appropriate inferential design.
6. **Quotations are illustrative.** Example excerpts were chosen because LLooM associated their response IDs with particular concepts. They demonstrate how a theme appears in the sample, not how dominant or representative it is.

## Conclusion

The selected analyses suggest that respondents evaluated AI policy through an implementation lens. Broad commitments—to accessibility, privacy rights, employment, or Indigenous leadership—were welcomed when they promised greater inclusion or control. Yet respondents consistently asked what would make those commitments usable in everyday life: relevant training, affordable access, understandable guidance, trusted safeguards, community participation, and mechanisms for adaptation.

The clearest cross-policy implication is that AI adoption should not be treated as a simple consequence of announcing programs or setting targets. Adoption is more likely when people and communities have the resources, capabilities, protections, and decision-making roles needed to engage with AI on terms that make sense in their own contexts.
